# human.md

Every word and pattern that makes a post read as machine-written.

This is the longest of the five files and the one that does the most work. An AI with good
instructions and no list like this still produces text a reader clocks in two seconds.

Most of what follows is not bad writing. It is writing that has been done ten million times
by a model, and readers have learned the shape.

---

## The rule this whole file serves

LinkedIn said it plainly in May 2026, through its Global Editorial VP:

> "It's ok to use AI to help you write, but your posts and comments need to represent your
> voice and your perspectives."

What it limits is content that **appears generated and lacks a clear perspective**. Both
halves. Its Chief Product Officer was blunter: *"AI and slop are not the same thing."*

So the practical version of the rule: **avoid generic writing, with or without AI.** A post
written entirely by hand with no number, no decision and no opinion can still read as slop.

Readers may have a "Seems like AI slop" option in a post's menu. I have no verified figure for
how often it is used, and no evidence that a flag by itself reduces a post's reach. Whatever
the platform's ranking does with it, a reader can still decide a post is not worth reading.
**So write for the reader.**

The most telling fact of the year: LinkedIn built an "enhance your post" AI writer, promoted
it, and removed it in August 2026, replacing it with something that *"proofreads your words,
but does not change your voice."* A company that sells AI tools decided generated posts cost
its feed more than the feature was worth.

## The one test that matters

**Could any other person in my field have published this exact post?**

If yes, it has no first-hand material in it, and every rule below is just a symptom of that.
If no, it survives most of these rules being broken.

Before drafting: write down the number, the decision, the thing that went wrong, or the
opinion someone could argue with. If there is none, the post is not ready, with or without a
model.

## 1. The formatting tells, which are mechanical and checkable

These are the ones that give a post away before anyone reads a word, and they come from the
copy button, not the writing.

Measured on 23 September 2026: the same eight LinkedIn prompts sent to ChatGPT and to Claude,
16 answers, every character counted.

| In the copied text | ChatGPT | Claude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Markdown bold `**` | 8 of 8 | 7 of 8 |
| Markdown list dashes | 5 of 8 | 0 of 8 |
| Em dashes | 7 of 8 | 0 of 8 |
| Curly quotes | 8 of 8 | 0 of 8 |
| Hidden characters | **0 of 8** | **0 of 8** |

**LinkedIn does not read Markdown.** Asterisks and list dashes paste in as themselves and sit
there in the published post. That gives a draft away more plainly than any watermark could,
and it reads as carelessness rather than as AI, which is worse.

- **Strip `**` and `-` list markers.** Bold becomes the bold letters LinkedIn keeps, or nothing.
- **Em dashes: replace with a full stop, a comma or a bracket.** Note the table: this is a
  strong tell for ChatGPT output and not one for Claude's. Either way, most people typing on a
  phone cannot easily produce one, so it reads as pasted.
- **Curly quotes** are the same story. Harmless alone, a cluster with the rest.

## 2. The five structural tells readers actually flag

Listed in order of how often they appear in flagged posts:

- **An opening that sets a scene without making a claim.** "In the world of B2B sales, trust
  is everything." Nothing to agree or disagree with, so nothing to read on for.
- **Lists of three where two would do.** A model reaches for three because three sounds
  complete. People write two things when they have two things.
- **"It's not X, it's Y."** A rhetorical move that sounds like insight and contains none.
- **A tidy lesson at the end.** The line that tells the reader what to conclude.
- **A question as the last line.** "What's your experience?" It reads as a request for
  engagement rather than a thought, and the reader can tell.

## 3. The structural tells, extended

**The three-part essay.** Observation, implication, three-item list, closing line restating
the observation as wisdom. It is the default shape a model falls into with no strong
instruction, and it is how nobody speaks.
*Instead:* one job per post. A draft containing a general observation, a personal story and a
tip list has three posts hiding in it.

**The moral at the end.** "And that changed everything." "Sometimes the simplest answer is the
right one." It is the sound of a writer who does not trust the story.
*Instead:* end on the last concrete thing that happened, or the decision made.

**The question as an opener.** "Ever wondered why some posts take off?" It asks the reader to
work before giving them a reason to. Measured against each writer's own typical post, the
question opener came last of eleven kinds.
*Instead:* a plain claim someone could argue with. In the same test it was the only opener
above average.

**The Named Concept.** "The 3-Lens Method." "The Clarity Loop." Naming a thing implies it has
been tested and taught. If it has not, the name is a costume.

**Lists where every item is the same length.** Six bullets, eleven words each, each starting
with a verb. Real lists are lumpy: one item has a number in it, one is three words.

**Emoji bullets.** 🚀 ✅ 💡 one per line. The single most common formatting pattern in
generated LinkedIn posts. Even people who like emojis rarely use them as a bulleting system.

**Hedged, unfalsifiable claims.** "This can often lead to improved outcomes." "Many
professionals find that..." A model hedges because it does not know. A sentence nobody could
disagree with is a sentence nobody will read.

**Explaining the obvious before the point.** "LinkedIn is a professional networking platform
with over a billion members..." The model is warming up. The reader has already scrolled.

**Enthusiasm with no object.** "Excited to share." "Thrilled to announce." It describes the
writer's feelings instead of giving the reader anything.

**The false confession.** "I'll be honest, I used to think X." where nothing is at stake.
Confession is a strong shape, which is exactly why a hollow one is obvious. A real one costs
the writer something: a number that embarrasses them, a decision they would rather not describe.

**Second-person advice the writer has not taken.** "You need to stop chasing vanity metrics."
Indistinguishable from the thousand other accounts doing it.
*Instead:* say what I did, and let the reader take the advice themselves.

**Symmetry where the subject is messy.** Every paragraph the same length, every section with a
heading, a conclusion mirroring the introduction. Real accounts of real work have uneven
parts, because some parts mattered more.

## 4. The vocabulary

**Delete on sight:** delve, tapestry, unlock, landscape, realm, elevate, seamless,
revolutionary, leverage, robust, game-changer, navigate (figuratively), foster, harness,
embark, testament, pivotal, crucial, vital, dive into, deep dive, treasure trove.

**Phrases:** "in today's fast-paced world", "in an increasingly digital landscape", "now more
than ever", "the key takeaway", "at the end of the day", "it's worth noting that", "let's be
honest", "here's the thing", "when it comes to".

**Headline shapes:** "Unleash your X", "Supercharge your Y", "Transform your Z", "The ultimate
guide to", "X things nobody tells you about Y".

These are not rare words someone happened to choose. They sit at the top of a model's
distribution for "write something impressive". A person writing fast reaches for shorter ones.

## 5. What not to waste time on

**Hidden characters.** Zero in all 16 answers tested, from both models. The invisible-watermark
story comes from an April 2025 report about narrow no-break spaces in two OpenAI models, which
OpenAI said was a training side effect, and which later output stopped showing. Check a draft
if it has been through a web page or a word processor, which do add them. Do not check for a
watermark that is not there.

**Removing a text watermark.** If a Claude output carries a watermark based on word choice,
stripping hidden characters or changing formatting would not remove it. A full rewrite can.
The useful work is still to put enough of my own material in the post that it stops
mattering where the first draft came from.

## The check before anything goes out

1. **Read it out loud.** A sentence that makes you wince is one of the above.
2. **Strip the Markdown.** No `**`, no `-` list dashes, no em dashes, no curly quotes.
3. **Find the first line.** Is it a claim someone could disagree with, or a scene being set?
4. **Find the last line.** Is it the point, or a lesson, or a question?
5. **Count the threes.** If a list has three items, does the third earn its place?
6. **The real test:** could any other person in my field have published this?

If the answer to six is yes, go back and put something in it that only I know. That is the
whole file in one sentence.
